A word before we start

I want to be honest with you about something. There are seasons where I look up and realize I have no idea where my week went. The calendar was full. I was busy. But if someone asked me what I was building toward, I'd have to pause longer than I'd like.

I think a lot of us are living in that gap. Full days, but scattered ones. And the thing is, busyness and faithfulness are two different things. Scripture actually has a lot to say about the difference.

This episode is an invitation to close that gap. To take a real look at how you're spending your time and ask whether those choices are ones you're making on purpose, with God in mind. Jason Van Dyke joins us to bring a framework that has helped him do exactly that. His book, Rules for (My) Life, came out of decades of journaling and a simple conviction: a good life doesn't just happen to you. You build it.

Grab a journal. Work through this slowly. And let's figure out together what it looks like to steward your time as an act of worship.

Anchor Verse

"Look carefully then how you walk, not as unwise but as wise, making the best use of the time, because the days are evil."

Ephesians 5:15–16 ESV

That phrase "making the best use of the time" is a marketplace word in Greek. It means to buy something back. To redeem it from being spent on lesser things. Paul is saying: your time will go somewhere. The work is deciding where, and doing it on purpose.

01
The Foundation

Your Days Were
Given to You

Before we talk habits or frameworks, we need to get the foundation right. Because how you see time will shape everything else. And Scripture gives us a starting point that changes the whole conversation.

Psalm 139:16 says God ordained every one of your days before you lived a single one. That means your time was given to you. You didn't create it. You received it. That one shift, from owner to steward, opens up a completely different way of living.

Stewardship is freeing, not just demanding. When your time belongs to God, you get to ask Him how to use it. You carry the responsibility of a steward, but you also carry the peace of someone who knows they are managing something that was already paid for.

There are two ways people lose their time. Overwork says your output is your worth, so you grind until something breaks. Overplay says your pleasure is your purpose, so you escape until the days are gone. Both start from the same place: forgetting that the time belongs to Someone else.

The world measures time by productivity. Scripture measures it by faithfulness. Paul does not say "make the most of your time" in the hustle sense. He says make the best use of it, which requires knowing what "best" actually means, and letting God define it.

02
The Practice

What Prayer Does
to Your Day

1 Timothy 2 is one of those passages that sounds gentle until you actually read it closely. Paul is giving us a practical framework for how a faithful day is built, and it starts somewhere most of us don't expect.

Paul starts with intercession. Pray for everyone, he says. For kings and all who are in authority. Why? So that we may live quiet and peaceable lives in godliness. Prayer is the architecture of the day, laid before anything else gets placed on it.

Quiet and peaceable is not passive. In the Greek, these words describe a life that is arranged well. A life with room to move. Prayer is how you create that margin. It reorients what matters before the demands of the day have a chance to tell you what matters instead.

Colossians 4:5 ties wisdom directly to how you use time. Walk in wisdom toward outsiders, making the best use of the time. The people around you are watching how you live. A life with intention and peace is a witness. A life that is constantly frantic and overextended tells a story too.

"Teach us to number our days that we may get a heart of wisdom."

Psalm 90:12

Numbering your days is an act of attention. It's the practice of taking your life seriously enough to ask how you're actually spending it, and humble enough to let God answer.

03
The Framework

Deciding How
You Want to Live

This is where Jason Van Dyke's work gets really practical. He spent years as a software developer before becoming a counselor, and somewhere in that journey, he started asking a question that changed everything for him: what would it look like to actually decide how I live, rather than just react to whatever comes next?

A rule for your life is a chosen principle, not a rigid law. Think of it less like a regulation and more like a commitment you made to yourself about the kind of person you want to be. Jason has spent years refining his, and Rules for (My) Life is the guide that came out of that process.

Deciding how to live is a deeply biblical act. Proverbs is full of this. Choose wisdom. Choose the path of the righteous. Walk with the wise. Scripture assumes that people of faith will bring intentionality to how they spend their days, and the people who do that well rarely stumble into it. They chose it.

Rest and saying no are the hardest disciplines for most people. Sabbath was commanded, which tells us something important: humans default toward endless motion. Learning to stop is a theological act. Every no you say to something lesser creates space for a yes that actually matters.

Faith belongs at the center of any framework you build. Personal rules without God become self-optimization, and that road has a ceiling. With God at the center, your framework becomes something closer to a daily liturgy, a structure that keeps returning you to what's real and what's eternal.

Journal prompts

Take these slowly. These are worth sitting with, maybe over a few days rather than all at once. Grab a journal and write out your honest answers. The goal here is clarity, and clarity takes a little space.

01

When you look at the last two weeks, where did your time actually go? Is that where you would have chosen to send it?

02

What does a "quiet and peaceable" life look like for you personally? What would need to shift in your week to get there?

03

Where are you most likely to let life happen to you rather than choosing it? What is one area where you want to be more intentional?

04

What is one rule you could write for your own life today? Something specific, something you actually want to live by. Write it down.

Your next step

Start with one rule.

You don't need a full life system by tomorrow. You need one thing. One commitment you make about how you're going to use your time, specific enough that you'll know whether you kept it or not.

Here are a few examples to spark something for you. Take one, adjust it to fit your actual life, and write it down somewhere you'll see it.

☀️

Protect your first hour. Before the phone, before email, before the day gets loud — spend the first part of your morning with God. Prayer, Scripture, quiet. Build the day on that foundation.

📖

Number one day each week. Take 10 minutes on Sunday to look at the week ahead. Ask God: where do I want to be intentional? What matters most? Let that answer shape how you plan, not the other way around.

🛑

Name one thing you're going to stop. Every good yes requires a no somewhere. Pick one way your time is going to something lesser and decide, specifically, to stop doing it. That space is where something better can grow.

✍️

Write your rule. Grab a journal and write one sentence: "I will ___." Make it specific. Make it yours. Then share it with someone who will ask you how it's going.
